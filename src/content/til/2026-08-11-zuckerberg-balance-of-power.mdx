Mark Zuckerberg's argument for why superintelligence should be distributed to everyone rather than concentrated in a few labs isn't primarily a technical safety claim — it's a political one. He argues there's no such thing as a "singular benevolent superintelligence" because humanity isn't a monoculture: any one system aligned to serve everyone's opposing values at once is impossible, so the real lever is making sure no single actor (a lab, a government, or an AI itself) holds a decisive advantage over everyone else.

The illustrations he uses are deliberately symmetric: a world where only one person has a superintelligent lawyer is unjust regardless of who's right on the merits, but a world where everyone has one is fairer; a world where only one actor has cybersecurity superintelligence is dangerous, but a world where the capability is widespread hardens every system instead. The safety mechanism isn't alignment of a single model — it's competing, roughly equal capability checking itself, the same logic used to justify democratic institutions.

It's a reframe worth noticing because it sidesteps the usual alignment debate (can we make one model provably safe?) in favor of a distribution debate (who gets to hold the capability at all), which changes what counts as evidence of progress — market share and access metrics instead of benchmark scores.

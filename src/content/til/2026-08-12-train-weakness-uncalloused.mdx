Josh Waitzkin's counterintuitive coaching move: when an elite performer has a flaw — say, a poker player's need for control — going straight at it in the arena where it's most costly is usually the least effective approach. By the time someone is world-class, they've built up defenses and workarounds around that flaw precisely so they can keep performing despite it. The weakness is real, but it's armored.

Instead he looks for a domain where the same underlying pattern shows up with no calluses on it at all — the same controlling poker player is probably controlling with their kids or their spouse, where there's been no professional need to compensate. Working the pattern there, where it's exposed and low-stakes, is easier to actually touch. Once it loosens in that undefended arena, it tends to generalize and "release" back into the professional one, even though no one worked on the poker game directly.

He treats deliberately uncomfortable practices like cold-water immersion the same way: not as a fitness routine, but as a stand-in arena for training one's relationship to resistance and discomfort in general, on the theory that the pattern is thematic rather than domain-specific — train it anywhere it shows up cleanly, and it shows up everywhere else too.
